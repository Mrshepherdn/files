"I can't afford to start a business."

This is the second biggest barrier to entrepreneurship — right after "I don't know where to start." And like the first one, it's based on a misunderstanding.

The truth is: **there is more money available than there are people to lend it to.** Funding is not the scarce resource. Understanding is.

## Understanding the Lender's Business

Here's something most people never think about: **lending is a business.** The lender's product is money. Their profit is interest. Their entire operation exists to find people to lend to — profitably.

That means lenders are not trying to say no. They're trying to say yes — to the right people, under the right conditions.

Their challenge? **Managing risk.** Every loan is a bet that the borrower will repay. The lender's job is to make that bet as safe as possible.

Once you understand this, everything changes. You stop seeing the lender as a gatekeeper and start seeing them as a **business with needs you can satisfy.**

## What Lenders Need From You

Every lender — whether it's a government-backed scheme or a high street bank — is looking for some combination of these:

### 1. A Bit of Good History
Your personal credit history. They want to see that you've managed money responsibly. This doesn't mean you need a perfect score — it means you shouldn't have recent defaults, CCJs, or bankruptcy. Most people's credit history is good enough.

### 2. The Ability to Repay
Can the business generate enough revenue to cover the loan repayments? This is where your **business plan and financial projections** matter. The lender needs to see that the numbers work — not just on paper, but stress-tested against realistic scenarios.

### 3. Skin in the Game
Some lenders want to see that you're putting something in yourself. It might be a cash contribution, personal savings, or assets. Not all funding routes require this — Start Up Loans, for example, are unsecured — but having some personal investment strengthens any application.

### 4. Business Viability
Is this a real business in a real market with real demand? For supported living, this is one of your strongest cards. The sector is:
- Publicly funded through local authority commissioning
- Experiencing a shortage of providers
- Structurally growing due to demographics and policy
- Backed by government strategy (deinstitutionalisation, community care)

A lender looking at a supported living business sees **low market risk and high demand.** That works in your favour.

## Funding Routes for Your Supported Living Business

### 1. Start Up Loans (Government-Backed)

**What:** Unsecured personal loans for new businesses, delivered through the British Business Bank.

**How much:** £500 to £25,000 per person. If applying as a group, each person can apply individually — meaning three co-directors could access up to £75,000 combined.

**Interest:** 6% fixed per annum.

**Repayment:** 1 to 5 years.

**Security:** None. These are unsecured loans. No collateral required.

**What you need:** A viable business plan, cash flow forecast, and evidence that you've thought through the business. You also receive 12 months of free mentoring.

**Why this works for Zundara ventures:** Your venture comes with a bank-grade business plan and financial projections already mapped to assessor questions. The documentation is built to satisfy exactly this type of application.

### 2. Growth Guarantee Scheme

**What:** Government-guaranteed business loans delivered through accredited lenders (high street banks, alternative lenders).

**How much:** Up to £2 million.

**Guarantee:** The government guarantees 70% of the loan to the lender — which means the lender's risk is significantly reduced, making approval more likely.

**Who qualifies:** Businesses with turnover up to £45 million. New businesses are eligible.

**Why this works:** For larger supported living operations — multiple properties, higher staffing — this scheme unlocks significant capital with reduced lender risk.

### 3. High Street Bank Business Loans

**What:** Standard business loans or overdraft facilities from major banks (Barclays, HSBC, NatWest, Lloyds, etc.).

**What they want:** Business plan, financial projections, evidence of market demand, personal guarantee in some cases.

**Why this works for Zundara ventures:** The financial models, business plans, and market analysis included with every venture are built to the standard banks expect. You're not walking in with a napkin sketch — you're presenting institutional-quality documentation.

### 4. Syndicate / Group Funding

**What:** Two, three, or more people come together to pool personal resources and jointly fund the business.

**How it works:**
- A venture costs £5,000
- Three people contribute £1,667 each — or less, if combined with a Start Up Loan
- Each person becomes a shareholder and director
- Governance documents define roles, responsibilities, and decision-making

**Why this matters:** This is the route that makes entrepreneurship accessible to people with **zero personal capital.** If you can't afford £5,000 alone, you don't need to. Bring a partner. Bring two. The business is structured to support group ownership.

**Example:** Three friends — a support worker, a nurse, and someone with business administration experience — each put in £1,667 and apply for individual Start Up Loans. Combined, they have the venture cost covered plus working capital for launch. Each brings a different skill set. The policies and procedures guide all of them.

### 5. Local Authority Grants

**What:** Some local authorities offer grants or funding support for new businesses, particularly in sectors where they have commissioning needs (like care).

**How to find them:** Check your local council's business support pages, LEP (Local Enterprise Partnership) websites, and organisations like Locality and Power to Change.

**Why this works:** If your local authority is short of supported living providers — which many are — they have a direct interest in helping you establish.

### 6. The King's Trust (Ages 18-30)

**What:** Enterprise programme for young people, offering funding, training, and mentoring.

**How much:** Grants and low-interest loans typically up to £5,000.

**Who qualifies:** Young people aged 18-30 who are unemployed or underemployed.

**Why this works:** A young person with care experience — even just a few months as a support worker — could use this route to fund a venture and launch their own business before they're 25.
